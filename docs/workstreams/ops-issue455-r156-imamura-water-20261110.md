## Substantive direct original research

**Primary**: Koji Imamura (2024), *The clean taste of coffee depends on the sodium content of its extraction water*, Food Science and Technology Research 30(2), 213–221, DOI `10.3136/fstr.FSTR-D-23-00129`, [J-STAGE original complete HTML](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/fstr/30/2/30_FSTR-D-23-00129/_html/-char/en). Released 2024-03-19. Directly read identity (publisher lines 12–39), Introduction 72–87, all methods 88–121, Tables 2/3 and primary water/sensory/sensor results 122–170, analytes/ions and sodium citrate perturbation 171–204, Table 4 and Conclusion 213–240.

Actual brew **one Arabica Brazil/Ethiopia mocha blend**, `28g` grounds with `515mL` at `78°C` in **Zojirushi drip coffee maker**, not pressured espresso. Five reported experimental water categories: melted-ice, local ground, local tap, melted-ice plus 0.5 mg/L NaClO, ultrapure. The narrative sometimes says *four* water types but lists five; no false count inferred. Six trained volunteers undertook blind categorical JCQA positive/negative taste checks; NOT six independent continuous espresso sensory scales, nor DIC's five 1–5 scores. TS-5000Z electronic taste-sensor, HPLC caffeine/chlorogenic and chromatography/cation measures were also reported.

In Table 3 melted-ice coffee collected **six positive clean checks** and water comparisons differed in total JCQA check-sum; ground/tap yielded fewer. In reported sampled brewed coffees sodium was 0.49/7.02/2.06/0.86 ppm while caffeine/chlorogenic acid differences were not statistically significant. In later sodium-citrate water perturbation, 0.75 ppm sodium citrate reduced positive clean checks from **4 to 2 of six**, and instrument saltiness shifted significantly while its other measured categories did not show significant changes. The two control baselines are distinct; avoid conflating the Table 3 6/6 clean with Table 4's 4/6 base. No inference of universal thresholds or personal causality from small categorical counts.
