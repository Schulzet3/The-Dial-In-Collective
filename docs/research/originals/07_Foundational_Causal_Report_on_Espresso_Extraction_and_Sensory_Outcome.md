# Foundational Causal Report on Espresso Extraction and Sensory Outcome

## Executive summary

The strongest conclusion from the available scientific literature is that espresso should **not** be modeled as a list of independent recipe knobs. It is better represented as a **coupled reactive-flow system** in which a few upstream choices determine the hydraulic state of a changing porous bed, and that hydraulic state governs mass transfer. Grind size and particle-size distribution, dose relative to basket geometry, puck packing/preparation, pressure or flow control, temperature, water chemistry, and the stopping yield are upstream inputs. Puck permeability/resistance, flow rate, local wetting, shot time, extraction evenness, and channeling are intermediate states. Extraction yield and beverage concentration are outputs. Sensory perception is a still-later output that depends not only on bulk extraction yield and concentration but also on the chemical composition of what was extracted and on how unevenly it was extracted. citeturn2view0turn24view0turn25view0turn26view0

This hierarchy matters for a recommendation engine because **shot time, puck resistance, extraction yield, and TDS should generally be treated as observations or state variables rather than interchangeable recipe controls**. For example, two shots lasting 30 seconds can have radically different flow histories and extraction distributions; two shots at the same brew ratio can behave differently when that ratio is reached by changing dose versus changing beverage yield; and two shots at the same nominal pressure can have different flow because the puck has a different permeability. Cameron et al.'s modeling and espresso experiments directly challenged the common assumption that time can be independently specified alongside dose, grind, pressure, and beverage mass. citeturn3view0turn3view1

A second foundational result is that **grinding finer has two opposed effects**. It increases particle surface area and generally speeds extraction from individual particles, but it also lowers puck permeability and increases hydraulic resistance. Above a critical fineness, espresso experiments show that the second effect can create sufficiently nonuniform flow that bulk extraction yield actually falls as the grind becomes finer. This "peak extraction" phenomenon is one of the best-established exceptions to traditional espresso heuristics. citeturn2view1turn3view0turn12search0

Particle-size distribution is at least as important as a single grind-size statistic. Smrke, Eiermann, and Yeretzian experimentally showed that the proportion of particles below 100 μm and the size of the main particle population both independently predict shot time. Added fines primarily changed permeability rather than providing the large extraction-efficiency benefit that might be expected from their extra surface area. citeturn24view0 A 2026 X-ray microtomography study provides a stronger physical basis: puck permeability is governed principally by connected pore volume and specific surface area, both of which depend on grind and packing. citeturn26view0

**Pressure is not an extraction-intensity dial.** It supplies a pressure gradient that drives flow, but the coffee bed itself is deformable. Recent pressure-controlled espresso experiments found approximately Darcy-like behavior at low pressures below about 5 bar but flow-rate saturation in the normal espresso-pressure regime as the puck compacted. In the authors' experiments, increasing pressure sufficiently far did not continue producing proportionally greater flow; the resulting pressure-flow curve was nonlinear. citeturn25view0turn25view5turn25view6 This makes fixed rules such as "more pressure extracts more" physically wrong.

Conversely, **flow rate is closer to a causal mass-transfer variable than shot time is**. At a fixed beverage yield, slower flow generally increases contact time and can increase the amount of soluble material transferred. A controlled 2023 espresso study varying flow, grind, and temperature found flow to have the strongest effect of those three manipulated factors on measured component extraction, although beverage mass itself produced still larger changes. citeturn11search0turn11search2 Yet very slow flow is not intrinsically desirable: it can be a symptom of an over-fine, highly heterogeneous puck in the regime where extraction evenness collapses. Cameron et al. and Smrke et al. together demonstrate why "longer shot = more extraction" is not a safe causal rule. citeturn3view0turn24view0

**Beverage yield is unusually powerful because it is both simple and directly interpretable.** With dose held fixed, allowing additional beverage to leave the puck normally increases the total mass of extracted solubles, hence extraction yield, while the later fractions of an espresso are progressively less concentrated. Thus a longer yield will ordinarily raise EY while lowering average beverage TDS, all else equal. The relationship between extraction yield, TDS, dose, and beverage mass is a mass balance rather than a sensory hypothesis. citeturn2view0turn25view0 James Hoffmann's professional explanation is consistent with this mechanism: in *Understanding Espresso - Ratio (Episode #2)*, he describes water as the solvent and explains at 03:47–04:00 that using more solvent generally increases extraction. citeturn21search1turn22search8

The useful accounting identity is approximately:

\[
\mathrm{EY}=\frac{\mathrm{beverage\ mass}\times\mathrm{TDS}}{\mathrm{dry\ coffee\ dose}}
\]

or, if brew ratio is defined as beverage mass divided by dose,

\[
\mathrm{TDS}\approx\frac{\mathrm{EY}}{\mathrm{brew\ ratio}}.
\]

This means **extraction yield and beverage strength cannot be treated as synonyms**. A longer shot can extract more total material yet be less concentrated. A shorter shot can have lower extraction yield yet higher TDS. citeturn2view0turn24view0

Temperature has a real chemical effect, but the evidence does **not** support treating temperature as a universally dominant extraction variable. In a controlled 2023 experiment spanning roughly 80–98°C, changing temperature by itself around the experiment's central flow/grind condition produced nearly overlapping extraction kinetics for the measured nonvolatile components, although the full factorial model found temperature and interactions to have statistically detectable effects. Earlier espresso work found meaningful differences in volatile and sensory composition between temperatures. The defensible conclusion is therefore that temperature can change kinetics and composition, but its magnitude and sensory direction are conditional; beverage yield, flow, and hydraulic uniformity can dominate it. citeturn11search0turn11search2turn7search8

The scientific basis for **preinfusion** is much weaker than its prevalence in modern espresso practice might suggest. Current physics strongly supports the mechanism: the first seconds of brewing involve displacement of air and CO₂, wetting, swelling, compaction, and establishment of a percolating water pathway. Waszkiewicz et al. observed roughly 5–10 seconds for this transient wetting/percolation process under their experimental conditions. citeturn25view0turn25view6 Jonathan Gagné's instrumented professional experiments similarly show that a meaningful Darcy-like puck resistance cannot be defined before saturation and that puck resistance changes dramatically during initial wetting. citeturn20search2 What remains poorly established is the stronger claim that any particular preinfusion protocol reliably raises EY or sensory quality.

Puck preparation has a similarly asymmetric evidence base. **There is good evidence that spatial packing matters, but weaker evidence that particular enthusiast techniques are universally superior.** Peer-reviewed work has demonstrated that deliberately changing particle placement through the depth of a puck changes and can stabilize flow. citeturn19view0 By contrast, two controlled studies found tamping force itself to have little or no detectable effect over the tested ranges once a puck was compacted. citeturn26view1turn3view2 Claims such as "deep WDT is always best" therefore remain below the evidentiary threshold for a foundational rule even though WDT is widely used by expert practitioners.

Water chemistry contains one of the clearest scientific disagreements. Hendon and colleagues' influential 2014 density-functional-theory work predicts strong interactions between dissolved Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ and coffee molecules, providing a plausible mechanism for mineral-dependent extraction. citeturn26view2 However, a 2024 experiment that directly compared Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ additions before versus after brewing found only limited changes in measured organic acids at concentrations typical of drinking water and broadly similar effects when salts were added after brewing. That result suggests that at least for those highly soluble acids, much of the water-chemistry effect may arise from post-brew complexation or perception rather than materially greater extraction from the grounds. citeturn17search12turn17search4

Finally, **bulk EY is not a sufficient objective function for an espresso engine**. Smrke et al. found different classes of volatile compounds increasing, decreasing, remaining unchanged, or showing non-monotonic relationships with EY. Their sensory component involved only one Q-grader and was explicitly not a blinded systematic sensory experiment, so its apparent optimum cannot be generalized. citeturn24view0 An engine should therefore optimize at least three distinct latent dimensions: **how much was extracted, how concentrated it is, and how evenly/chemically selectively it was extracted**.

**Bottom-line architecture:** the engine should model espresso approximately as

**grind/PSD + dose/basket + puck prep + water + temperature + pressure/flow/preinfusion → evolving puck permeability/resistance and wetting → flow field and residence time → local extraction distribution → bulk EY + beverage concentration + chemical composition → sensory outcome.** citeturn24view4turn25view0turn26view0

## Variable glossary and causal relationship map

The glossary below distinguishes **primitive controls**, **derived recipe variables**, **hydraulic states**, and **outcomes**. That distinction should be explicit in the data model because changing an actuator is not equivalent to observing its consequence. citeturn2view0turn25view0

| Variable | Operational meaning for this research module | Causal status |
|---|---|---|
| **Dose** | Dry mass of ground coffee placed in the basket. It also affects puck depth for a given basket and packing state. citeturn2view0turn26view0 | Primitive control |
| **Beverage yield** | Mass of beverage collected when the shot is stopped. It determines how much solvent-throughput is allowed before termination. citeturn2view0turn22search8 | Primitive stop condition |
| **Brew ratio** | Beverage yield divided by dry dose. It is mathematically derived from two controls and is **not an independent physical mechanism**. citeturn2view0 | Derived recipe variable |
| **Grind size** | Position of the main ground-particle population, with corresponding changes in specific surface area and permeability. A single median does not describe the full PSD. citeturn24view0turn26view0 | Primitive control/proxy |
| **Grind distribution** | Full particle-size distribution, especially fines fraction and main-particle population. It influences packing, connected pore space, surface area, and flow resistance. citeturn24view0 | Primitive material property |
| **Shot time** | Elapsed time required to reach the stopping criterion. At fixed yield it is approximately the integral consequence of flow history, not an independent extraction force. citeturn3view0turn24view0 | Derived observation |
| **Brew temperature** | Temperature history of water and coffee bed. It influences diffusion, solvent properties, viscosity, volatility, and chemical kinetics. citeturn11search0turn7search8 | Primitive control, imperfectly observed |
| **Pressure** | Pressure difference driving fluid through the bed. It simultaneously affects flow and mechanically deforms the puck. citeturn25view0 | Primitive actuator / hydraulic input |
| **Flow rate** | Rate at which water/beverage moves through the system. It sets solvent delivery and residence/contact time but can either be controlled by the machine or emerge from pressure and resistance. citeturn11search2turn25view0 | Control or intermediate state |
| **Preinfusion** | Initial low-pressure/low-flow wetting stage before the principal extraction phase. It changes saturation, swelling, gas displacement, and initial resistance. citeturn25view0turn20search2 | Control strategy |
| **Puck resistance** | Hydraulic opposition to flow produced by puck thickness, permeability, viscosity, packing, saturation and deformation. It changes dynamically during a shot. citeturn25view0turn26view0 | Latent/intermediate state |
| **Puck preparation** | Operations determining the spatial arrangement and packing density of grounds before brewing: distribution, de-clumping, leveling, tamping and related procedures. citeturn19view0turn26view1 | Upstream control |
| **Extraction evenness** | Spatial dispersion of local extraction throughout the puck. Equal bulk EY does not imply equal local EY. citeturn24view4turn12search0 | Latent outcome/state |
| **Channeling** | Preferential flow through relatively permeable pathways, bypassing less-permeable regions and producing heterogeneous residence times and extraction. citeturn24view4turn12search0 | Failure mode / dynamic state |
| **Extraction yield** | Mass of dissolved coffee material transferred into the beverage divided by initial dry coffee dose. citeturn2view0turn24view0 | Bulk outcome |
| **Beverage concentration** | Concentration of soluble coffee material in the beverage, commonly estimated refractometrically as TDS. citeturn24view0turn25view0 | Bulk outcome |
| **Water chemistry** | Dissolved ions and buffering chemistry of brew water, especially alkalinity and Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺/Na⁺ chemistry. It can affect extraction chemistry and post-extraction perception. citeturn26view2turn17search12 | Primitive material property |
| **Basket geometry** | Basket diameter/cross-sectional area, depth, wall profile, hole distribution/open area and relationship to dose/headspace. These set boundary conditions for the porous bed. citeturn24view4turn24view3turn26view0 | Hardware constraint |

```text
                                   │
                      ┌────────────┴─────────────┐
                      ▼                          ▼
              EXTRACTION EVENNESS          CHANNELING
                      │                          │
                      └────────────┬─────────────┘
                                   ▼

                                   │
           ┌───────────────────────┴──────────────────────┐

   EXTRACTION YIELD                              BEVERAGE CONCENTRATION

           └──────────────────────┬───────────────────────┘
                                  ▼
                  composition + aroma + texture
                                  │
                                  ▼
                           SENSORY OUTCOME

BEVERAGE YIELD = stop condition on the flow integral.
BREW RATIO = beverage yield / dose.
SHOT TIME = elapsed time required to reach that stop condition.
```

This representation follows the experimentally supported view of an espresso puck as a **dynamic porous medium** rather than a fixed resistor. Water first infiltrates and swells the bed; pressure can compact it; dissolution and particle rearrangement alter permeability; and heterogeneous extraction can feed back into heterogeneous flow. citeturn25view0turn12search0turn26view0

The feedback loop is particularly important:

\[
\text{local flow}\rightarrow\text{local extraction}\rightarrow\text{local porosity/permeability change}\rightarrow\text{more local flow}.
\]

Lee, Smith, and Arshad developed a theoretical espresso model in which this positive feedback can amplify small initial permeability differences until one flow path becomes highly extracted while another remains underextracted. Their work provides a mechanistic explanation for the experimentally observed collapse of extraction at excessively fine settings, although the model itself is theoretical rather than a direct visualization of channel formation. citeturn12search0turn12search8

A second feedback is mechanical:

\[
\text{pressure}\rightarrow\text{puck compaction}\rightarrow
\text{pore-space reduction}\rightarrow\text{resistance increase}\rightarrow
\text{modified flow}.
\]

The 2026 pressure-controlled work directly supports this mechanism and explains why extrapolating a simple linear Darcy relation through the whole espresso-pressure range is unsafe. citeturn25view0turn25view6

These labels are judgments from the source base rather than statistical probabilities.

Two evidence patterns deserve special emphasis.

First, **the strongest variables are not necessarily those with the most familiar barista heuristics**. Puck permeability, PSD, flow history and stopping yield have unusually strong mechanistic foundations, whereas exact pressure, time and preinfusion targets are much more context dependent. citeturn24view0turn25view0turn26view0

Second, **sensory evidence is consistently weaker than hydraulic and chemical evidence**. Much of espresso science measures EY, TDS, caffeine, chlorogenic acids, organic acids, VOCs, flow or pressure rather than conducting adequately powered, blinded sensory trials. The recommendation engine should attach lower confidence to sensory translations than to physical predictions unless matched-EY/matched-TDS sensory data exist. citeturn24view0turn26view1turn7search8

The interaction table implies that a practical engine should distinguish between **interventions** and **observations**. For a conventional pressure-limited pump machine, grind is an intervention and flow is mostly an observation. On a true flow-controlled machine, flow becomes the intervention and pressure becomes substantially an observation of puck resistance. Barista Hustle's technical description of flow profiling makes this same control-system distinction: actual flow profiling requires continuous flow measurement and machine adjustment, whereas a fixed restrictor merely imposes a restriction and allows flow to change as puck resistance evolves. citeturn20search15

This also explains why recipes are not fully portable between machines. A stated "9 bar, 30-second, 1:2" recipe fails to specify the initial wetting state, flow trajectory, pressure at the puck rather than pump, basket geometry, PSD, spatial packing, temperature history, or degree of hydraulic heterogeneity. Recent pressure measurements even show that pressure loss through the brewer itself varies with flow, so pump-side and basket-side pressure need not be identical. citeturn25view0

## Expert consensus and important disagreements

Across the scientific and high-quality professional sources, there is a fairly strong **consensus on the causal skeleton**, even though the preferred practical recipes vary.

**Grind is simultaneously a chemical and hydraulic variable.** Cameron et al., Kuhn et al., Smrke et al., the 2026 permeability study, and Hoffmann's professional explanation all agree on the central mechanism: smaller particles provide more surface area but also more resistance to percolating water. Hoffmann's *Understanding Espresso - Grind Size (Episode #4)* describes at 00:52 onward the simultaneous increase in accessible surface area and flow resistance from finer grinding, and at approximately 03:41 discusses the increased risk of channeling at high resistance. Those points are consistent with the later peer-reviewed evidence, though the peer-reviewed papers should carry greater weight in the engine. citeturn22search3turn22search12turn3view0turn24view0

**Yield is one of the most dependable controls of extraction progression.** Hoffmann's ratio explanation at 03:47–04:00 is aligned with the formal mass-transfer literature: additional solvent enables additional extraction, while the time-resolved science shows that marginal espresso fractions become progressively dilute. citeturn22search8turn25view6

**Time is diagnostically useful but causally ambiguous.** Contemporary scientific work and professional instruction converge on the idea that shot time is best interpreted in context. Cameron et al. explicitly argue against independently navigating brew time; Smrke et al. demonstrate high extraction efficiency in short shots; and Hoffmann's educational series treats time as something affected by grind and flow rather than a stand-alone chemical force. citeturn3view0turn24view0turn20search0

**Pressure, flow and resistance form a coupled system.** The modern scientific evidence substantially strengthens what pressure/flow-profiling practitioners have observed. Gagné's instrumented resistance analysis found major transient resistance changes; Barista Hustle distinguishes actual flow control from restriction-based control; and the 2026 poroelastic work directly demonstrates nonlinear pressure-flow behavior caused by puck deformation. citeturn20search2turn20search15turn25view0

**Distribution matters more than exact tamp force once the puck is adequately compacted.** The direct tamping experiments of Kuhn et al. and Cameron et al. found no detectable or appreciable effect from force over their tested ranges. The peer-reviewed stratification study, by contrast, shows that spatial particle arrangement can materially change flow repeatability. citeturn26view1turn3view2turn19view0 This supports the narrower consensus that *where the grounds are and how uniformly they are packed* matters more than continually increasing tamp force.

**EY and TDS are useful measurements, not complete definitions of quality.** Smrke et al.'s VOC results are especially important: different volatile groups followed different trajectories as EY increased, demonstrating experimentally that higher EY does not simply scale every flavor-active compound upward together. citeturn24view0

The most consequential disagreements are as follows.

**Finer grinding: monotonic versus non-monotonic extraction.** The traditional rule says finer grinding raises extraction because of surface area and contact time. That rule is valid over part of the range and is supported by component-specific work such as Kuhn et al. citeturn26view1 The Cameron experiments, however, show a clear fine-grind regime where EY falls, while Lee et al. provide a theoretical instability mechanism that can produce that behavior. citeturn3view0turn12search0 For an engine, the disagreement is resolved by treating the relation as **piecewise/non-monotonic**, not by choosing one side.

**Nine bar as an optimum.** Historical espresso practice and older sensory/chemical experiments often center on approximately 9 bar; Andueza et al. found favorable characteristics at their 9-atmosphere condition compared with 7 and 11. citeturn7search15 Newer physics shows no fundamental optimum at 9 bar: pressure-flow behavior depends on bed properties, and high pressure can compact the bed rather than simply drive proportionally more useful flow. citeturn25view0 The engine should therefore treat approximately 9 bar as an historically common operating point, not a law of extraction.

**Temperature as a major extraction lever.** Earlier espresso sensory studies found significant temperature effects and condition-specific preferred temperatures. citeturn7search8 Schmieder et al.'s newer controlled kinetics, however, found a surprisingly small isolated temperature effect on several measured compounds across a broad tested range under the central conditions, while flow and beverage mass were substantially more influential. citeturn11search0turn11search2 The disagreement is likely more about **what outcome is measured**—bulk/component extraction versus volatiles/sensory—and interactions, rather than temperature being either wholly important or irrelevant.

**Fines as beneficial surface area versus hydraulic liability.** Simple extraction theory suggests fines should extract quickly because of their large surface-area-to-volume ratio. Smrke et al.'s espresso experiments instead found their major practical effect to be reduced permeability/increased shot time, with only marginal evidence that the added surface area improved extraction efficiency. citeturn24view0 The engine should consequently avoid a generic "fewer fines = better" or "more fines = higher extraction" rule.

**Water minerals as extraction enhancers versus post-brew modifiers.** Hendon's 2014 computational chemistry gives a strong mechanistic reason for Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺ to interact differently with coffee molecules. citeturn26view2 Bratthäll, Figueira, and Nording's 2024 analytical experiment found that, for several organic acids at ordinary water concentrations, pre-brew mineral addition had little advantage over adding the same ions after brewing. citeturn17search12 This directly warns against using the famous cation-binding model as though it were a measured universal espresso-extraction law.

**Preinfusion as mechanistically sensible versus empirically optimized.** Physics strongly supports pre-saturation/wetting as a distinct phase. citeturn25view0 Professional sources commonly infer that gentler preinfusion should reduce channeling; Barista Hustle describes that as a principal use of pressure profiling. citeturn20search7 Yet there is little high-quality controlled evidence establishing a universal preinfusion duration, pressure, flow or sensory benefit. The mechanism should become part of the model; a preferred recipe should not.

**WDT and other modern prep methods.** Gagné's measurements and modern professional practice give credible evidence that puck preparation changes resistance and repeatability. citeturn20search2 But the peer-reviewed literature has not yet established a universal ranking of deep WDT, shallow WDT, blind shaking, distributor tools, puck screens and similar methods under matched conditions. The engine can store those features; it should not assign them deterministic quality scores.

**Universal "ideal EY" zones.** Cameron et al. discuss traditional extraction ranges, but newer work demonstrates that chemical and aroma composition do not move uniformly with EY, while Smrke et al.'s specific sensory result was explicitly not designed to establish a universal optimum. citeturn2view0turn24view0 A future recommendation engine may discover coffee- and preference-dependent EY objectives, but this foundational module should not hard-code one.

Several familiar statements should **not yet become engine rules**.

**"Target 25–30 seconds."** This is a historical/professional operating convention, not a causal optimum. Smrke et al. demonstrated substantial extraction in less than 10–15 seconds, while Cameron et al. showed that very fine, slow shots can have declining EY. citeturn24view0turn3view0 Time can remain a useful warning threshold for a particular calibrated recipe, but it should not be globally scored.

**"Target 9 bar."** An older controlled study found favorable results near 9 atm in its test conditions, but 2026 physics demonstrates that pressure response depends on puck deformation and permeability. citeturn7search15turn25view0 Nine bar can be a default machine context, not a universal taste optimum.

**"A 1:2 ratio is ideal."** Brew ratio is derived, and identical ratios reached through different dose/yield combinations are not hydraulically equivalent. No source reviewed here establishes a universal sensory optimum at one ratio. citeturn3view1turn26view0

**"Higher EY is always better."** The volatile composition of espresso does not scale uniformly with EY, and evenness matters independently of the bulk mean. citeturn24view0turn24view4

**"EY between approximately 18–22% is objectively balanced."** Historical brewing-control conventions are useful descriptive reference points, but the espresso evidence does not justify converting that range into a universal sensory objective. Cameron et al. discuss conventional extraction ranges; Smrke et al. explicitly caution that their coffee-specific sensory optimum cannot be generalized. citeturn2view0turn24view0

**"Finer is better until the shot becomes too slow."** The failure mode is more subtle. Bulk EY can already be declining because of heterogeneous flow; shot time alone does not locate the extraction optimum. citeturn3view0turn12search0

**"If a shot is slow, it is overextracted."** A fine, channeled shot can be slow while significant puck regions remain poorly extracted. citeturn3view0turn12search0

**"If a shot is fast, it is underextracted."** Fast shots in Smrke et al. achieved 17–18% EY and more than 80% of their recipe's observed maximum extraction. citeturn24view0 Speed alone is insufficient.

**"More fines always increase extraction."** Added fines mainly acted as a permeability modifier in Smrke et al.'s experiment; the predicted surface-area advantage was small. citeturn24view0

**"Fewer fines are always better."** Fines are important to puck permeability, and removing them can move a shot into a completely different hydraulic regime. The current evidence does not define a universal optimal fines fraction. citeturn24view0turn26view0

**"Harder tamping increases extraction."** Controlled measurements did not detect a meaningful extraction effect from tamp-force variation within their tested ranges. citeturn26view1turn3view2

**"WDT always increases EY."** Modern practice and instrumented professional work make the hypothesis credible, but the peer-reviewed literature reviewed here does not establish a universal WDT effect size or superiority to other contemporary distribution methods. citeturn20search2turn19view0

**"Longer preinfusion always reduces channeling."** Saturation physics makes the proposition plausible, but no universal controlled relationship between preinfusion duration and channeling/EY/sensory quality has been demonstrated in the cited evidence. citeturn25view0turn20search7

**"Higher pressure extracts more."** At fixed resistance it primarily drives more flow; at espresso pressure it can compact the bed; and faster flow can lower extraction at a fixed beverage yield. citeturn25view0turn3view2

**"Hotter water always gives higher EY."** A controlled broad-temperature experiment found the isolated temperature effect surprisingly small under one central condition, and temperature's larger effects may involve compound selection and volatility rather than a universal bulk-EY increase. citeturn11search2turn7search8

**"Magnesium-rich water extracts more desirable acids."** Hendon's molecular model gives a plausible cation-binding mechanism, but the 2024 pre-/post-brew experiments found little evidence that ordinary Mg²⁺ or Ca²⁺ levels materially increased extraction of the tested acids. citeturn26view2turn17search12

**"Visible bottomless-portafilter spraying measures channeling severity."** Internal preferential flow is the scientifically relevant phenomenon; the sources reviewed here do not validate visible sprays as a quantitative proxy for local extraction distribution. citeturn24view4turn12search0

**"A high-flow basket automatically increases extraction."** Basket and perforated-disc changes measurably alter chemical extraction and boundary conditions, but there is no universal direction independent of dose, grind, flow and geometry. citeturn24view3turn26view0

The safest first-generation decision logic is therefore **directional and diagnostic rather than recipe-prescriptive**. For example:

```text
Observed low EY
    |
    +-- Is flow very fast / resistance low?
    |      -> plausible insufficient residence/contact
    |      -> finer grind or lower flow may increase EY
    |
    +-- Is flow very slow / resistance high?
           -> do NOT automatically grind finer
           -> test for fine-grind extraction collapse / unevenness
           -> coarser grind may paradoxically increase EY

Observed low TDS
    |
    +-- Is EY also low?
    |      -> extraction problem is plausible
    |
    +-- Is EY adequate/high?
           -> concentration is primarily a ratio/yield problem,
              not necessarily an extraction problem

Observed long shot
    |
    +-- Stable/even hydraulic behavior?
    |      -> may simply reflect lower flow
    |
    +-- High variability / signs of permeability instability?
           -> long time is not evidence of good extraction

Pressure change requested
    |
    -> estimate puck resistance and control mode first
    -> predict resulting FLOW, not taste directly
```

Every branch above follows the distinction between causal upstream controls and downstream observations found in the physical literature. citeturn3view0turn24view0turn25view0

## Unanswered research questions and complete source list

For an espresso recommendation engine, the largest remaining scientific gap is **not another search for a universal recipe**. It is identifying which measurable signals allow the system to infer the latent hydraulic and extraction state of the puck.

The highest-priority unanswered question is whether **extraction evenness can be predicted non-destructively from pressure, inlet flow, outlet mass-flow and resistance trajectories**. Mathematical models strongly predict local extraction heterogeneity, and modern X-ray approaches can observe pore structure, but a validated mapping from easily collected machine telemetry to local EY distribution does not yet exist. citeturn24view4turn12search0turn26view0 Such a mapping would be transformative because it could allow the engine to distinguish a slow, even shot from a slow, partially channeled shot without relying on visual heuristics.

A closely related question is what **resistance trajectory predicts impending channeling**. Gagné's measurements show large resistance evolution, while Waszkiewicz et al. show that swelling, compaction and dissolution all change flow over time. citeturn20search2turn25view0 Future experiments should determine whether normalized features such as post-saturation resistance slope, pressure-flow hysteresis or late-shot flow acceleration predict local extraction variance.

**Preinfusion requires a genuine factorial experiment.** The field needs experiments independently varying preinfusion flow, maximum pressure, duration, pause/bloom duration and transition rate while holding dose, PSD, basket, temperature and final yield constant. Measurements should include EY, TDS, pressure/flow trajectories and preferably spatial extraction or CT imaging. Current evidence establishes transient saturation physics but not an optimum control strategy. citeturn25view0turn20search2

**Modern puck-preparation methods need peer-reviewed head-to-head testing.** Deep WDT, shallow WDT, blind shaking, no distribution, wedge/leveling tools, puck screens and paper filters should be tested with randomized shot order, sufficient replication, multiple grinders/PSDs and blinded sensory evaluation. Existing peer-reviewed work proves that spatial particle arrangement can affect flow, but it does not rank the contemporary techniques most users actually choose between. citeturn19view0turn26view1

**Basket geometry needs to be connected to whole-shot EY and sensory data rather than selected chemical compounds alone.** Current work establishes geometry-dependent local flow and chemical composition, while the 2026 permeability model gives the necessary porous-medium framework. citeturn24view4turn24view3turn26view0 A recommendation engine particularly needs cross-basket transfer functions: given the same coffee, PSD and desired flow regime, how should dose/grind be transformed between diameters and outlet geometries?

**PSD needs better representation.** Smrke et al. demonstrate that fines share and main-particle size both matter, implying that a single D50 or grinder setting is insufficient. citeturn24view0 Future engine-facing research should determine the minimal set of PSD descriptors that predicts permeability and extraction robustly across grinders—potentially fines mass/volume fraction, main mode, width/skew and shape descriptors—without requiring laboratory particle imaging for every shot.

**Flow-versus-pressure profiling requires matched-outcome sensory work.** Physics can tell us how profiles change hydraulic conditions, but it cannot determine whether consumers or trained panels prefer one profile after EY and TDS are held constant. The 2026 pressure work establishes nonlinear mechanics; it does not establish an ideal sensory profile. citeturn25view0

**Temperature needs experiments that isolate direct chemistry from indirect flow effects.** The apparent disagreement between older sensory work and newer extraction-kinetics work could be resolved by simultaneously controlling puck temperature, flow, pressure and final EY/TDS while measuring both nonvolatile solutes and aroma. citeturn7search8turn11search2

**Water chemistry needs espresso-specific, blinded sensory and compositional experiments at realistic concentrations.** The 2024 divalent-cation work is especially valuable because it challenges a widely accepted mechanism for organic acids, but it does not settle effects on less-soluble compounds, buffering, aroma, texture or sensory perception. citeturn17search12turn26view2

**The field needs matched-EY/matched-TDS tests of extraction evenness.** Without them, the statement that a homogeneous 20% EY espresso tastes better than a heterogeneous 20% EY espresso remains highly plausible but incompletely quantified. Models predict the underlying heterogeneity; bulk refractometry hides it. citeturn24view4turn12search0

**Marginal extraction deserves more attention than total shot time.** Time-resolved work shows that early espresso is highly concentrated and later fractions become weak, while VOC behavior can be nonlinear. citeturn25view6turn24view0 An engine may ultimately benefit more from estimating the composition or TDS of the **next gram of beverage** than from estimating total final EY alone.

**Online sensing is an important frontier.** The SCA has highlighted ongoing Hendon-associated research aimed at extracting more chemical information from espresso in real time rather than relying only on final TDS/EY measurements, underscoring that this remains an active research problem rather than a solved control system. citeturn0search5turn0search11
