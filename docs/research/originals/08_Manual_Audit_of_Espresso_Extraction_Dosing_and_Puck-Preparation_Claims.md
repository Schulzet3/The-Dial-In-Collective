# Manual Audit of Espresso Extraction, Dosing, and Puck-Preparation Claims

## Scope and audit method

I treated this as a **pre-scale quality-control exercise**, not as an attempt to maximize the number of espresso “facts.” The audit focuses on claims that are especially likely to be repeated as universal rules when the underlying evidence is actually conditional: grind size, extraction yield, dose and beverage ratio, shot time, pressure, puck preparation, fines, channeling, and Ross Droplet Technique-style bean wetting.

The evidence base was deliberately weighted toward primary technical sources: peer-reviewed research articles, author manuscripts of peer-reviewed work, and the newest controlled espresso-flow work available. A particularly useful recent source is Waszkiewicz et al.’s *Under pressure: poroelastic regulation of flow in espresso brewing*, which used a café-grade machine over approximately 1–12 bar but explicitly warns that its model is coarse-grained, that the long-time saturated regime is its strongest domain, and that grind, roast and dose were not systematically varied. citeturn47view2turn48view4 Moroney et al.’s 2019 PLOS ONE study is valuable for extraction uniformity, but an important limitation is easy to miss: its experimental rig used a 59-mm chamber at 90°C, **60 g of coffee and about 1 L of water at 250 mL/min**; the authors themselves state that although the geometry resembled espresso equipment, the quantities and brew duration were closer to drip-filter brewing. citeturn51view0

That distinction matters. A result obtained in an espresso-shaped experimental chamber is not automatically a conventional-espresso result, and a conventional espresso result is not automatically a turbo-shot result.

I also encountered an instructive source-access problem. Cameron et al.’s 2020 *Matter* paper, *Systematically improving espresso: insights from mathematical modeling and experiment*, is the foundational work repeatedly cited in the later technical literature on the non-monotonic fine-grind regime. Its bibliographic existence and role are directly documented by subsequent peer-reviewed work, but its publisher full text could not be retrieved through the research interface during this audit. citeturn48view4turn47view1 I therefore **did not substitute press articles or coffee-media retellings as if they were the paper itself**, and I downgrade conclusions requiring details unique to that paper. That is exactly the sort of source-boundary rule that should be enforced before generating hundreds of entries.

For the “conventional versus turbo” column below, **“conventional tested” means the cited experiment actually used a café-style, relatively high-resistance espresso preparation**. “Turbo not established” means the source does not directly test a deliberately coarser, lower-resistance, faster-shot regime. I do not assume a result transfers between the two simply because both are called espresso.

## Confidence framework

Your confidence taxonomy works well, with one important implementation detail: the label should attach to the **carefully qualified conclusion**, not to the catchy version of the claim. Otherwise a system can produce the absurd result “High confidence: 9 bar is universally optimal” merely because hundreds of derivative sources repeat it.

| Confidence | Operational meaning in this audit |
|---|---|
| **High** | Supported by controlled research or multiple strong independent primary sources, within explicitly stated conditions |
| **Moderate** | Consistent evidence plus a plausible mechanism, but important generalization or causal uncertainty remains |
| **Preliminary** | Limited direct testing, a single credible experiment, or an expert practice not isolated experimentally |
| **Anecdotal** | Individual or community practice without adequate controlled comparison |
| **Inferred** | Logical or model-based synthesis that has not itself been directly demonstrated experimentally |
| **Conflicted** | Credible evidence or legitimate interpretations materially disagree, or different regimes produce opposite-looking results |

Two additional fields should be mandatory in any future database: **Evidence Type** (`observed`, `modeled`, `inferred mechanism`, `sensory`, `expert preference`) and **Applicability** (`coffee`, `roast`, `grinder`, `basket`, `dose`, `pressure`, `ratio`, and conventional/turbo regime). The audit below shows why.

## What survives the audit

Several conclusions emerge much more strongly than particular recipes.

First, **espresso behaves as a coupled hydraulic and extraction system rather than a collection of independent knobs**. Particle size affects both accessible surface and bed resistance; fines can accelerate local mass transfer while restricting flow; pressure can deform or reorganize the bed; dissolution itself changes the porous structure over time. Moroney et al. show this coupling between particle size, extraction and hydraulic resistance, while Waszkiewicz et al. directly observe pressure-dependent flow that cannot be represented by a single constant-permeability Darcy relation over the whole espresso pressure range. citeturn51view0turn48view4 Micro-CT and fluid-dynamic work by Mo et al. independently shows that coffee-bed microstructure meaningfully affects permeability and tortuosity, and that pre-/post-extraction structures differ; their proposed erosion explanation, however, remains a mechanism layered onto the imaging rather than something that should be conflated with the image itself. citeturn54view0

Second, **the grind/extraction relationship is not globally monotonic**. “Finer gives more extraction because it gives more surface area” is locally reasonable but globally incomplete. Very fine grinding can enter a regime in which flow heterogeneity overwhelms the expected surface-area benefit. The existence of that reversal is grounded in the experimental literature summarized by Lee et al.; their particular two-pathway explanation successfully reproduces the behavior but remains mechanistic modeling. citeturn47view1 This is an ideal example of how an entry should contain separate fields for `Observed Result = non-monotonic extraction` and `Proposed Mechanism = flow/extraction feedback producing pathway imbalance`.

Third, **measurement and preference must be separated aggressively**. TDS is beverage concentration; extraction yield estimates how much dry coffee mass entered the beverage. Neither quantity measures “deliciousness.” The latest pressure work explicitly calls TDS a scalar reduction of chemically complex coffee, while the extraction-uniformity literature shows that two pucks cannot be assumed equivalent merely because their average extraction number is the same. citeturn48view4turn51view0 An instruction such as “maximize extraction” is therefore a process objective, not automatically a sensory recommendation.

Fourth, **the 18–22% rule is a particularly good stress test for citation laundering**. In Moroney et al., the historical evidence described for the 18–22% preference region is rooted in filter coffee. The authors then explain that the range became broadly accepted by baristas for espresso. citeturn51view0 A derivative article can easily collapse those two statements into “studies show espresso tastes best between 18 and 22%,” and a language model can repeat that simplification hundreds of times until it looks authoritative. The source does not warrant that wording.

Fifth, **equipment and roast conditions can reverse the practical importance of an intervention**. Méndez Harper and Hendon obtained a large RDT effect on espresso made from their dark-roasted Temascaltepec, under a precisely specified EK43/Black Eagle/7-bar/94°C/18-g-to-45-g setup, but found no significant corresponding brewing change in the light-roast version of that coffee. citeturn52view0 The properly scoped entry is therefore not “RDT increases extraction by 16%.” It is closer to: “In this dark-roast/EK43 experiment, small pre-grind water addition altered the delivered PSD, slowed flow and increased beverage concentration by about 16%; the light-roast comparison showed little brewing effect.” citeturn52view0

Finally, **conventional espresso evidence cannot be silently converted into turbo-shot rules**. The 2026 pressure study intentionally prepared a café-style espresso around 18.5 g, a roughly 2:1 beverage ratio and approximately 32 s around the conventional pressure region. citeturn47view2 Its authors explicitly say systematic changes in grind, roast and dose remain to be explored. citeturn48view4 Therefore its pressure curve, pause behavior, WDT procedure, tamp setting or 1:2 baseline should not be written as though individually validated across deliberately lower-resistance, faster recipes.

## Failure modes the audit exposes

The most serious problem is not outright fabrication. It is **progressive loss of qualifiers**.

A primary paper might say:

> one coffee + one grinder + one basket + one pressure regime → observed result.

A secondary summary tends to become:

> this parameter causes the result.

A generated database entry then becomes:

> this is how espresso works.

After enough repetitions, an inferred mechanism or recipe preference acquires the appearance of high confidence even though no new experiment has occurred. The RDT result demonstrates why the omitted qualifiers matter: roast level materially changed whether the brewing effect was detectable. citeturn52view0 The 2026 pressure paper likewise explicitly restricts its conclusions by acknowledging unmodeled early multiphase behavior, spatial heterogeneity, fines migration and channel formation, and by noting that parameter variation remains future work. citeturn48view4

A second failure mode is **citation by proximity**. The fact that a controlled research paper uses WDT, a blind shaker, 20-kg tamping and a 1:2 recipe does not make that paper experimental evidence that each practice is optimal. Those were preparation controls in Waszkiewicz et al., not randomized independent variables. citeturn47view2 The same error appears when fixed 196-N tamping in an RDT study is recycled as evidence for ideal tamp pressure. citeturn52view0

A third is **mechanism inflation**. Lee et al. are admirably careful: non-uniform extraction is a “potential explanation,” and their model demonstrates that the mechanism *can* generate the experimental trend. citeturn47view1 Waszkiewicz et al. similarly distinguish observed pressure-flow behavior from their coarse-grained poroelastic model and enumerate alternative/omitted effects. citeturn48view4 A database that stores only the conclusion and not `Evidence Type` will almost inevitably turn those mechanisms into facts.

A fourth is **sensory substitution**. “Increased TDS,” “higher extraction,” “slower flow,” “more uniform local extraction,” and “more reproducible” are not synonyms for “tastes better.” The PLOS paper itself describes both historical preference-based brewing charts and their limitations, while the latest physics paper stresses how much chemistry is discarded when coffee is represented simply as TDS. citeturn51view0turn48view4 Any recommendation using words such as *better, sweeter, balanced, cleaner, more delicious,* or *optimal* should require either direct sensory evidence or an explicit `Preference/Expert Opinion` designation.

A fifth is **regime collapse**. High-resistance conventional espresso, very-fine stalled/channeling regimes, and low-resistance turbo-style shots should not occupy one undifferentiated parameter space. The finding that finer grinding eventually lowers extraction is specifically about crossing a critical regime; it is not evidence that coarser is always better. citeturn47view1 Likewise, a non-linear pressure-flow relationship measured in a particular conventional puck cannot automatically predict the behavior of every substantially coarser puck. citeturn47view2turn48view4

## Revised research instructions before scaling

The manual audit does indicate that the research instructions should be tightened before generating hundreds of entries. I would use the following specification.

**Every claim should be atomic.** “RDT reduces static, improves extraction, makes espresso sweeter, and is best for light roasts” must be split into separate claims because those propositions have different evidence. In the actual study, static reduction is strongly measured, increased espresso concentration is strongly demonstrated only under particular dark-roast conditions, and universal flavor improvement is not demonstrated; the light-roast result actually weakens the broad extrapolation. citeturn52view0

**Every entry should store the exact evidence type.** At minimum:

`Observed experiment | Sensory experiment | Model result | Proposed mechanism | Expert practice | Community anecdote | Logical inference`

A model reproducing an observation is not itself observation. Lee et al.’s pathway mechanism and Waszkiewicz et al.’s poroelastic mechanism illustrate why this must be machine-enforced. citeturn47view1turn48view4

**Every technical claim should carry an applicability envelope.** The mandatory fields should be:

`coffee/origin`, `roast`, `coffee age if reported`, `grinder/burrs`, `particle-size information`, `dose`, `basket geometry`, `beverage yield/ratio`, `temperature`, `pressure or flow profile`, `preinfusion`, `puck preparation`, `water if reported`, and `conventional/turbo/other`.

When a field was not controlled or reported, store **“not reported”**, not an inferred default. RDT, for example, should retain EK43 stock 98-mm burrs, dark Temascaltepec, 18.0 g → 45.0 g, 94°C, 7 bar and 2-s preinfusion with the result. citeturn52view0

**Require a source-entailment sentence.** Before accepting an entry, the system should answer:

> “What, precisely, did the authors measure that establishes this claim?”

If the answer is “they used WDT in their method,” the system must reject “WDT improves flavor” as unsupported by that source. Waszkiewicz et al. used both shaking and WDT, so that paper cannot even isolate the independent effect of either method. citeturn47view2

**Add a mandatory scope-check field:** `BroaderThanEvidence = Yes/No`. An entry should automatically be `Yes` when it removes an experimentally relevant qualifier. Examples from this audit include turning “dark roast on an EK43” into “all coffee,” turning “standard café-style 9 bar” into “optimal pressure,” or turning filter-derived preference ranges into universal espresso thresholds. citeturn52view0turn47view2turn51view0

**Keep extraction claims separate from sensory claims.** A useful formal rule would be:

> No entry may infer `flavor better/worse` solely from TDS, EY, flow rate, shot time, or pressure unless a sensory study or explicitly identified expert preference is supplied.

This prevents the common EY→taste and RDT→taste errors exposed above. TDS is explicitly an incomplete scalar representation of coffee chemistry. citeturn48view4

**Require disagreement preservation rather than consensus synthesis.** A system should not average contradictory observations into a bland “best practice.” Fines are an example: they can increase accessible surface and extraction rate while also increasing bed resistance; that tension is real and should remain in the entry. citeturn51view0turn52view0 Similarly, finer grinding can first aid extraction and later reduce it. citeturn47view1 `Conflicted` should therefore be a legitimate endpoint, not a temporary state that the model feels obliged to resolve.

**Never promote confidence through repetition.** Confidence should rise only when **new independent evidence** is added. Ten articles copying the same experiment still count as essentially one evidentiary lineage. A later model explaining an earlier experiment adds mechanistic support, but it does not become a second independent observation of the phenomenon. This is particularly important for the Cameron→Lee fine-grind literature, where Lee et al. explicitly build a model to explain an earlier experimental result. citeturn47view1

**Treat expert recommendations as scoped preferences unless experimentally validated.** When an expert says “I prefer,” “I dial in,” or “this tastes best to me,” preserve that wording conceptually as `Expert Preference`. Do not rewrite it as “best practice.” Even research protocols can incorporate expert-derived preparation practices without testing them; the 2026 pressure study’s shaking/WDT protocol is a concrete example. citeturn47view2

**Require a regime field before accepting a parameter recommendation.** An entry about grind, pressure, time, ratio or dose should state `Conventional`, `Turbo`, `Both directly tested`, or `Transfer inferred`. “Both” should be allowed only when both regimes were actually tested or when the proposition is sufficiently fundamental and independently validated. The current primary evidence does **not** justify casually transferring the conventional 18.5-g/2:1/~32-s pressure experiment to turbo shots. citeturn47view2turn48view4

The strongest result of this audit is therefore methodological rather than a new espresso recipe: **the research system should store conditions, evidence type, sensory status, regime applicability, disagreement, and source entailment as first-class data—not as optional prose notes.** The audited literature repeatedly shows that the underlying science is conditional: fines can help one variable while hurting another, pressure changes both the hydraulic load and the bed, very fine grinding can reverse its expected extraction benefit, and interventions such as RDT can have strongly roast-dependent outcomes. citeturn47view1turn48view4turn51view0turn52view0

Under that structure, an **Inferred** mechanism cannot become **High** merely because it is copied into fifty downstream entries; a **Preliminary** recipe preference cannot masquerade as a universal law; and a legitimate **Conflicted** conclusion can remain conflicted until experiments actually resolve it.
